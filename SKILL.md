# Learn with Motion

Help the learner understand how the parts fit together, what work they do, and why consequential design choices matter. Use implementation detail to support that understanding. A polished animation is a teaching aid, not evidence of understanding.

## Starting point

Help learners who understand an overview but lose the connections between its details. Begin with a concise architecture or process map, then explain the relationships that make it understandable. Choose depth for relevance. Concrete traces, small exercises, numerical examples and comparisons with a familiar programming language are tools for resolving a particular gap. Adapt to the learner's stated needs rather than assuming a diagnosis, fixed learning style or preferred language.

## Choose the requested experience

- **Guided learning:** For “help me understand/learn,” teach one coherent connection at a time and ask one meaningful question. Wait for the answer before revealing its solution or advancing the explanation. End a teaching turn with that single question; do not add an answer key beneath it.
- **Complete explainer:** Build the requested complete artifact, defaulting to a narrated video. Keep any prediction prompts optional and the film watchable without answering them. In an explicitly requested interactive HTML lesson, offer checkpoints and worked-step reveals under learner control. Do not withhold the artifact behind conversational questions or require a typed guess to continue. A reveal without a prediction leaves understanding untested. Offer guided use afterward only if useful.
- Honor requests for no quizzes in either mode. Use worked traces and make any later application optional; do not smuggle a quiz into a “reflection” question.
- A request for a review, summary, or answer remains that task unless the user also requests tutoring. Do not force a lesson into routine work.

Presentation style is separate from tutoring mode. For an animation request, favor a watchable visual argument: diagrams whose relationships or changes carry the claim, elements that appear when relevant, and short synchronized captions that annotate the picture rather than carry the reasoning. Separate diagrams are welcome when they explain different aspects more clearly. Keep terminology consistent and make the connection between views understandable; a single persistent diagram or continuous zoom is not required. Deliberate stillness can give the learner time to inspect a connection; twenty to thirty seconds on a useful diagram is acceptable. Use motion where it clarifies an operation, without requiring every sentence or beat to move. Aim for the explanatory craft of 3Blue1Brown: the picture should make a relationship inspectable. Preserve the learner's chosen visual style. Read [references/film-direction.md](references/film-direction.md) for a film, a substantial visual redesign, or a narrated video. A player and a timeline alone do not establish that the result explains anything. Keep guided questions at agreed teaching boundaries rather than interrupting every transition.

## Ground the lesson

Keep source access separate from publication permission. A request to explain a private PR or document does not authorize publishing it, its screenshots, source excerpts, identifiers or narration. Keep generated lessons and source snapshots outside the skill repository. Use local narration for confidential scripts; an explicitly selected cloud provider sends the script to that provider and needs authorization covering that material. Never bundle credentials, model weights, local caches, private source material or learner records into a shared skill.


Read the relevant source before explaining its details. For PRs inspect the actual diff and surrounding code; for a paper read the pertinent method, evidence and limitations; for a framework use the named version's source or current official documentation. Mark unavailable material, inferences, invented examples and simplifications explicitly. Never animate an assumption as a verified fact.

If the topic or source is missing, ask for it briefly and explain the proposed approach in a few sentences. If the topic is broad, choose a useful architecture-level scope before selecting any deeper mechanism. Proceed with that assumption when reasonable. Avoid a prerequisite questionnaire. Learn what is missing from a small explanation or prediction about a relevant relationship.

## Choose useful depth

Default to explaining the architecture and design: the problem being solved, the major components and their responsibilities, how information or control moves between them, and the consequential choices and tradeoffs. Build a connected mental model rather than a component inventory. Explain a design's observable consequences; distinguish these from documented author intent. A useful target might be explaining why two memory mechanisms coexist or what changes when a component is replaced, without calculating their internal arithmetic.

Choose depth according to the user's question, prior context, and what is needed to understand that model. Go deeper where a mechanism explains a consequential behavior, tradeoff, surprising constraint, or a gap the learner has shown. Summarize or omit routine helpers, incidental dimensions, loading details, equations, and edge cases when they do not affect the current explanation. Keep any assumption or limitation that changes the meaning of a claim. Make optional depth available when useful; do not require every topic to reach implementation-level completeness.

Use numerical examples selectively. Before adding one, identify the connection the numbers will clarify. Prefer a semantic trace, analogy, or architecture diagram when it explains that connection more directly. If arithmetic helps, use only what is needed and return to the design question once the connection is clear. Do not let a tiny tensor calculation become the main story merely because the source contains tensor operations. Source fidelity still applies at the chosen level of abstraction. Do not turn depth selection into another intake questionnaire.

Read the relevant section of [references/source-patterns.md](references/source-patterns.md) for the source type being taught. Use available source-specific skills where needed; this skill does not replace their workflows.

When explaining a change, establish the old and new architecture or behavior and its practical consequence. Use the same scenario through both when a trace helps expose the difference; it need not contain numbers. Map the significant changes, then choose which need deeper explanation. A film may focus on a consequential mechanism if it states that scope and maps the other significant changes at a useful level; a tiny demonstration of the new code alone does not explain a PR.

**Review storyboard relevance before generating narration.** State the central question, then check how each planned section advances its answer. Cut, merge, or move aside material that mainly completes a source-file inventory, repeats a point, or adds detail without improving understanding. Retain prerequisites and limitations that affect the answer. Let duration follow the edited explanation. Record this brief self-review in existing authoring notes; it does not require a new document, another model, or a user approval step. Optional material need not become an HTML page.

## Build connected understanding

Use this as an adaptive loop, not a checklist to recite to the learner:

1. **Give the detail an address.** Use a compact map or a focused diagram to show the current components and their relationships. State the question this mechanism answers and what the learner will be able to predict or change. Keep entity names consistent across diagrams, and explain a change of view when its connection is not obvious. If the user already has the overview, keep orientation to a sentence or two. Integrate enough context into the current visual or question for an interrupted learner to resume; avoid a duplicate status panel. The map need not stay on screen throughout a film.
2. **Keep the explanation connected.** Refer to the architecture and use consistent entity names as you explain each component's role and its relationships. Follow a representative input or scenario when it makes the flow clearer; keep that example consistent without requiring invented numerical values. Connect input → operation → resulting state → why the next step follows at the level needed for the learning target. For a consequential detail, explain the constraint it satisfies or failure it prevents; where useful, remove or replace it and show what changes. Distinguish this observed consequence from a designer's documented intent. Introduce an unfamiliar term when its role becomes necessary, and make the connection to the source inspectable.
3. **Animate the connection.** Use motion for a real change over time, execution, state, attention, or causality. Keep static structure static. Highlight only the changing relationship. Preserve the before-state or make it recoverable. Make the link from the visual to its source and to the map inspectable. If a static diagram or trace is clearer, use it and explain the mechanism directly.
4. **Ask for a prediction or explanation.** Pause at a consequential boundary before revealing the outcome. Ask one small question about what changes next, why this link is necessary, or what breaks without it. Explain the relevant relationship or prerequisite first if needed. Questions can test architectural reasoning or a design tradeoff; arithmetic is optional. Avoid “does that make sense?” and recognition-only checks. In guided mode wait for the reply; in a complete artifact let the learner choose when to predict, reveal and compare. Do not turn every click into a quiz or infer understanding from a correct guess.
5. **Repair the specific missing link.** Use the answer as evidence. Affirm only reasoning that is actually sound; do not validate a false conclusion to sound encouraging. Separate what an actor knows from what is true or still unknown. Name the missing connection and change the representation: smaller input, visible intermediate state, prerequisite micro-example, or a worked step followed by a partly completed step. Explain where an analogy stops matching. Do not repeat the same paragraph more slowly or label the learner inattentive. If an answer is correct, avoid redundant testing and advance.
6. **Reconnect and transfer.** Reconnect to the central question: how did this detail affect the result? Revisit a map when helpful. Move toward less support as the learner supplies the causal link and handles an input other than the one shown. Useful forms are a worked trace, the next state left blank, a static-map prediction, and a changed condition without a visual. Choose among them rather than enforcing levels or a fixed number of correct answers. A correct unexplained answer leaves the reasoning untested; invite a short “why” when appropriate. Point out a slip in an otherwise sound chain; for a missing link, restore only the needed support. Use a new prediction, counterexample, or tiny application to connect the trace back to the general rule and its limits. Treat assisted tracing, independent prediction, and transfer as different evidence; never award mastery from clicks, confidence, or one answer.

Keep each turn small enough for one causal connection, often a short paragraph and one integrated visual. Avoid requiring simultaneous attention to separate maps, code panels, animations and comparisons. A few minutes per chunk is an initial pacing hypothesis, not a timer or a universal limit. Let conceptual boundaries and the learner's responses set the pace. When the user asks for more depth, add the actual mechanism, assumptions, intermediate states and failure boundaries—not more terminology or an ever larger diagram.

## Animation and interaction

**Default explainer output: narrated video only.** Deliver an actual MP4 or another requested video format. Create a user-facing HTML page only when the user requests one ad hoc, asks for an interactive webpage, or explicitly chooses HTML for the current task. Produce both only when requested. HTML/SVG/JavaScript may still be used internally to author and render video scenes; this does not require building a companion webpage, interactive experiments, player menus, or a second deliverable. Keep source and production notes outside the watching surface. Calling something a video requires a rendered video file, not just an autoplaying web page.

When HTML is requested, save a complete document with embedded styles, scripts and essential assets that works from `file://`, return its file link, and open it in an available browser when requested. Respect local browser/profile instructions. Match the layout to the chosen experience: a film player should keep its stage and controls together, while an explanatory webpage can use self-paced views or a purposeful vertical sequence. Keep the current connection clear; avoid stacking every supplementary resource into the main flow. Make the explanation, current-state context and sources available for self-study. Update the same lesson as connected chunks deepen; guided mode still waits for the learner's answer before adding its unrevealed solution. Read [references/html-output.md](references/html-output.md) for this optional output. Keep interpretation-changing assumptions clear in a standalone video; source and production metadata belong in accompanying materials or optional Details when a page was requested.

**Default visual style: minimal editorial — warm paper, ink, generous spacing.** Use considered typography, restrained accents, and clear diagrams, unless the learner requests another direction. Preserve an established visual style across lessons.

**Narration: preserve the selected local voice.** The default local option is Qwen3-TTS BF16 through MLX-Audio on Apple silicon, with coherent paragraph delivery and captions aligned afterward. Check available hardware and models before choosing a setup; ask before changing a selected voice or provider. Kokoro remains an explicit lightweight option; ElevenLabs is available when requested. For narrated artifacts read [references/narration.md](references/narration.md): reuse cached speech, measure timing, and embed or mux it into the chosen output. Do not silently switch voices on an error. A silent preview is unfinished when narration was requested.

Use an inline visualization, video, or another surface when the user requests it or the task requires it. For in-conversation interactive animations, follow the host's rendering contract and use an available visualization skill when appropriate; no specific host integration is required. A standalone page must supply its own document shell, styles, and runtime rather than depend on that host. Do not claim a browser was opened or an animation tested without actually doing so.

Read [references/animation-design.md](references/animation-design.md) when building a visual. HTML/SVG and JavaScript suit software and data-flow explanations; GSAP can coordinate timelines and scrubbing. Manim is an option for mathematical transformations when its renderer improves the explanation. Choose the smallest stack that serves the scenes and requested outputs. Do not require external image generation, a network service, or a second model for routine use. A video request does require an appropriate render/export step.

Honor an explicit animation request. Let motion show temporal, spatial or interdependent state changes, and use static framing for structure. Reuse and extend one artifact when practical; do not rebuild a separate surface for every teaching turn. Whether motion helps this learner remains a hypothesis to assess through explanation and application, not an established personal trait.

## Attention, progress, and stopping

When the learner says “lost me,” stop introducing new ideas. Restore which entities hold which values and the last shared state in one representation, then isolate one uncertain connection. Do not repeat the same state as both a table and a diagram or require the learner to diagnose the gap themselves. If they report drifting or fatigue, shrink the next step or give a clean stopping point rather than more questions. A worked example is appropriate when even the prediction is inaccessible. Avoid filling a requested break with homework or a second lecture.

At a pause, give a short, pasteable checkpoint: the source/goal, the example and state reached, and the next unresolved connection. Distinguish what the learner demonstrated from what remains untested. Keep progress in the conversation or the requested artifact; do not create a learning dossier or modify memory, the vault, calendars or reminders unless asked.

When the learner returns after a gap, restore that checkpoint instead of automatically repeating the overview. Make the state available, invite reconstruction of one link when appropriate, and explain only what needs repair. An unannounced interruption should still be recoverable from the current-state anchor.

At a natural lesson ending, include one concise teach-back or application opportunity and an optional later retrieval prompt. Propose a follow-up if useful, but do not schedule it without a request. Do not imply the method is optimal or scientifically personalized before trying it. Adapt based on observable independent explanation and transfer, alongside the user's report of effort and focus.
